MASTER PROMPT: Professional Executive Infographic for Presentations
Act as an expert executive communications strategist, presentation designer, and infographic specialist.

Create a polished, visually engaging, executive-level infographic suitable for a business demo, leadership presentation, stakeholder briefing, or strategic discussion.

The infographic should communicate key information quickly, tell a compelling story, highlight important insights, and make recommended actions easy to understand.

1. Overall Design & Style
Use a clean, modern, professional corporate design.
Design for a 16:9 landscape presentation slide.
Use a consistent visual hierarchy, balanced spacing, and clearly defined panels.
Make the infographic visually appealing without overcrowding it.
Use concise, impactful language rather than lengthy paragraphs.
Incorporate appropriate icons, visual indicators, and subtle graphic elements.
Maintain consistent typography, colors, alignment, and panel styling throughout.
Ensure all text is readable when projected on a presentation screen.
2. Header Banner
Create a full-width dark navy blue header banner across the top.

Include:

Title: A compelling, concise title that captures the presentation's central theme.
Tagline: A short, meaningful subtitle that reinforces the primary message.
Icons: Appropriate professional icons that visually complement the topic.
Use white text against the dark navy background. Make the title prominent and the tagline secondary.

3. Infographic Layout
Organize the infographic into THREE horizontal rows.

TOP ROW – Three Panels
Panel 1: Executive Summary

Summarize the overall situation, objective, or initiative.
Highlight the most important message.
Include key facts, outcomes, or business objectives.
Panel 2: Why It Matters

Explain the business significance.
Highlight the impact, urgency, opportunity, or risks.
Connect the topic to organizational priorities and stakeholder value.
Panel 3: Where We Stand

Describe the current state or baseline.
Highlight progress, accomplishments, maturity, or status.
Use metrics, indicators, or visual status cues where appropriate.
MIDDLE ROW – Three Panels
Panel 4: Strengths

Highlight existing capabilities, successes, advantages, and positive momentum.
Emphasize what is working well.
Use positive visual indicators and relevant icons.
Panel 5: Gaps We Own

Identify challenges, weaknesses, limitations, and areas requiring improvement.
Be transparent, constructive, and accountable.
Avoid assigning blame.
Clearly distinguish known gaps from assumptions.
Panel 6: Path Forward

Present the recommended direction and strategic response.
Highlight priority actions and opportunities.
Show how identified gaps will be addressed.
Focus on practical, achievable next steps.
BOTTOM ROW – Two Panels
Panel 7: Phased Roadmap

Present a logical progression of activities or milestones.
Organize the roadmap into meaningful phases, such as:
Phase 1: Assess / Stabilize
Phase 2: Improve / Build
Phase 3: Implement / Expand
Phase 4: Optimize / Sustain
Adapt the number and names of phases to the subject matter.
Include milestones, expected outcomes, and timing when information is available.
Use a horizontal timeline or connected visual progression.
Panel 8: Takeaways

Summarize the three to five most important messages.
Highlight the desired outcome, decision, or action.
End with a clear, memorable closing statement.
Make the final message action-oriented and executive-friendly.
4. Visual Design Requirements
Header: Dark navy blue with white title and tagline.
Main body: White or very light neutral background.
Panels: Clearly separated cards with subtle borders, soft shadows, or light background variations.
Use a coordinated professional color palette:
Navy blue: Leadership, strategy, and direction.
Blue: Progress, capabilities, and initiatives.
Teal: Opportunities and forward movement.
Amber: Attention areas and improvement opportunities.
Green: Positive outcomes and accomplishments.
Gray: Supporting information and neutral context.
Include one or two appropriate icons per panel.
Use charts, arrows, progress indicators, timelines, or simple diagrams where they communicate information better than text.
Avoid decorative graphics that do not add meaning.
5. Content Adaptability
The subject matter and actual content will change between presentations.

Therefore:

Treat the eight panel headings as the default framework, not rigid content requirements.
Adapt the examples, insights, metrics, recommendations, and roadmap to the specific presentation topic.
Preserve the overall three-row, eight-panel structure unless explicitly instructed otherwise.
Do not invent statistics, achievements, timelines, or commitments.
If information is missing, use clearly identified placeholders or omit unsupported details.
Avoid repeating the same information in multiple panels.
Ensure each panel contributes a distinct part of the overall story.
6. Communication & Storytelling
The infographic should tell a coherent story:

What is happening?
Why does it matter?
Where are we today?
What are we doing well?
What needs improvement?
What should happen next?
How will we get there?
What should the audience remember or decide?
Use strong headlines, short supporting statements, and meaningful visual cues.

7. Final Output
Produce a presentation-ready infographic with:

One full-width dark navy header banner.
Three panels in the top row.
Three panels in the middle row.
Two wider panels in the bottom row.
Eight clearly identifiable panels in total.
Consistent icons and visual styling.
Strong executive-level storytelling.
A polished, balanced, professional 16:9 landscape composition.
Important: Prioritize clarity, visual impact, and executive readability over the amount of information included. The finished infographic should look like a professionally designed consulting presentation, not a collection of text boxes.
